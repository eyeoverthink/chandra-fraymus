# Discussion 2 — Law, Political Power, and the Long Accomplishment of Removal

**Prompt:** *Worcester v. Georgia* affirmed Cherokee sovereignty, but removal happened anyway. Using this contradiction as a starting point, explain what it reveals about the relationship between law and political power in this field. Then connect it forward to allotment: how did the Dawes Act use the language of "reform" and "citizenship" to accomplish what removal had accomplished by force?

## I. The Contradiction Is Not a Paradox — It Is the Design

*Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) is usually taught as a court doing the right thing and being ignored. Reading the opinion itself shows something more uncomfortable: the Court's affirmation of Cherokee sovereignty and the removal that followed it were not in tension. They were produced by the same premise.

Consider what the Court actually says. Worcester, a missionary, was indicted for "residing, on the 15th July, 1831, in that part of the Cherokee Nation attached by the laws of the State of Georgia to that County, without a license or permit from the Governor," and sentenced "to hard labour in the penitentiary for four years" [2]. The Court reversed, holding that "[t]he extraterritorial power of every legislature being limited in its action to its own citizens or subjects, the very passage of this act is an assertion of jurisdiction over the Cherokee Nation" [2].

But the ground of that holding is stated plainly in the same opinion: "discovery gave title to the government by whose subjects, or by whose authority it was made, against all other European governments, which title might be consummated by possession" [2]. That formulation is lifted from Marshall's earlier opinion in *Johnson v. McIntosh* (1823), which held that "the rights of the original inhabitants were in no instance entirely disregarded, but were necessarily to a considerable extent impaired" — they were "admitted to be the rightful occupants of the soil," but "their rights to complete sovereignty, as independent nations, were necessarily diminished," and "their power to dispose of the soil at their own will ... was denied by the original fundamental principle, that discovery gave exclusive title to those who made it" [12]. The United States, Marshall wrote, "maintain[s], as all others have maintained, that discovery gave an exclusive right to extinguish the Indian title of occupancy, either by purchase or by conquest" [12].

So the architecture is this. **Discovery gives the United States the ultimate title. Occupancy gives the tribe a right to possession that the United States alone can extinguish.** Cherokee sovereignty in *Worcester* is real — but it is a *residual* sovereignty, carved out of a title the Court has already assigned to the federal government. The Court in 1831 had already said the quiet part aloud, describing the Cherokee as "domestic dependent nations" in "a state of pupilage," their relation to the United States resembling "that of a ward to his guardian" — even while conceding the Cherokees "are a State" and "have been uniformly treated as a State" [3].

That is why the contradiction resolves. When Georgia asserts jurisdiction, the Court says no: that power belongs to the federal government, not the state. When the federal government decides to extinguish occupancy — by treaty, by purchase, or by force — there is no legal barrier left, because the Court has already held that the power to extinguish is exactly what the United States possesses [12]. *Worcester* protected Cherokee sovereignty **from Georgia**, not from the United States. Georgia's noncompliance and Jackson's non-enforcement are usually told as a story of law defeated by politics [2][7]. That reading is too generous to the law. The law had already conceded the dispositive question.

**What this reveals about law and political power:** in this field, law does not stand outside power, adjudicating it from above. Law is one of power's instruments — and its characteristic move is to convert a political decision into a legal premise, so that the decision no longer has to be argued for. "Discovery gave title" is not a finding; it is an assertion of European priority dressed as a rule of property, resting on the observation that it "was a right which all asserted for themselves, and to the assertion of which by others all assented" [12]. Legality here means agreement among the colonizing powers. The nations whose land it was are not parties to the agreement. Once that premise is installed, every subsequent legal victory for Indigenous nations operates *inside* it — and so cannot reach the premise itself.

The Removal Act confirms the pattern. Its §7 even provides that nothing in the act "shall be construed as authorizing or directing the violation of any existing treaty between the United States and any of the Indian tribes" [6]. The statute promises more legality, not less, at precisely the moment it authorizes dispossession.

## II. Allotment: The Same Work by Other Means

The Dawes Act did not reverse removal. It completed it — and it did so by taking the land that removal had *left* to Indigenous nations and dissolving the legal form in which it was held.

**The mechanism, in the statute's own words.** The General Allotment Act of February 8, 1887, directs that patents issue under which "the United States does and will hold the land thus allotted, for the period of twenty-five years, in trust for the sole use and benefit of the Indian," after which the United States "will convey the same by patent to said Indian, or his heirs as aforesaid, in fee, discharged of said trust and free of all charge or incumbrance whatsoever" [13]. Section 5 then authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to negotiate "the purchase and release by said tribe ... of such portions of its reservation not allotted," with those lands "held by the United States for the sole purpose of securing homes to actual settlers" in tracts "not exceeding one hundred and sixty acres to any one person" [13]. Proceeds are held in the Treasury and "shall be at all times subject to appropriation by Congress for the education and civilization of such tribe or tribes" [13].

Read that sequence as a machine. Allotment individualizes the land. The unallotted remainder becomes "surplus," available to settlers. And the sale money is placed in an account the tribe cannot spend, dedicated to its own "civilization." Every step is legally regular.

**The language of reform.** The Dawes Act did not present itself as dispossession. General Fisk told the Lake Mohonk Conference of the Friends of the Indian in September 1887 that "the passage of the Dawes Bill providing for the allotment of lands in severalty, the extension of law over the Indians, are the beginning of a new epoch in Indian affairs" [14]. Professor C. C. Painter, an abolitionist turned reformer, described the allottee as stepping "out of his undifferentiated, impersonal tribal relation into one of individualized, responsible citizenship," so that "[w]hen we make him a citizen, we recognize his manhood with all its inherent rights under the Declaration of Independence and Constitution" [14]. He cast allotment as liberation from "the old policy of enforced isolation, legalized pauperism and inevitable idleness and debauchery," and predicted that "[t]he reservation walls being down, and the restraining power of the agent broken," the allottee and his children would "become a race of wanderers and beggars" unless met with education [14].

Senator Dawes's own metaphor is the most revealing line in the record: "It seems to me this is a self-acting machine that we have set going, and if we only run it on the track it will work itself all out, and all these difficulties that trouble my friend will pass away like snow in the spring time, and we will never know when they go: we will only know they are gone" [14]. He is describing a mechanism, and he is right that it is one. The "difficulties" he expects to disappear are the Indian nations as political entities.

**Citizenship as the solvent.** Section 6 declares that any Indian "who has voluntarily taken up within said limits his residence, separate and apart from any tribe of Indians therein, and has adopted the habits of civilized life, is hereby declared to be a citizen of the United States" [13]. The Wikipedia summary of the provision puts the condition bluntly: citizenship came with having "adopted the habits of civilized life," meaning having "lived separate and apart from the tribe," and it was conferred "without in any manner impairing or otherwise affecting the right of any such Indian to tribal or other property" [17].

Note the condition. Citizenship is not offered *to* the nation; it is offered *out of* it — to the individual who has left. The legal effect is to dissolve the tribal relationship as the price of the rights of a citizen. And the claim that this "impair[s] ... no right ... to tribal or other property" is exactly the kind of sentence the field has learned to distrust, for the same reason §7 of the Removal Act should be distrusted [6][17].

**Why this accomplishes what force accomplished.** Removal moved nations bodily to clear land. Allotment dissolved the *communal holding* that made land defensible in the first place, so the land could pass without anyone being marched anywhere. The BIA's own account of the result: "Between the inception of the policy and its end in 1934 over 90 million acres passed out of Indian ownership and control," and allotment "gave rise to the problem of fractionation that persists to this day" [15]. A BIA briefing states the land base fell from 138 million acres in 1887 to 48 million acres in 1934 [16]. A U.S. Senate hearing record on Umatilla land consolidation records the same event as "91 million acres or two-thirds of the Indian land base ... lost" by 1933 [19]. Whichever figure one prefers, the magnitude is the same: roughly two-thirds of the land that Indigenous nations retained after removal was gone within a single generation of the allotment policy.

**The bridge back to the Cherokee.** The Dawes Act expressly did *not* apply to Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Seminole, Miami, Peoria, Osage, Sac and Fox, the Seneca of New York, or the Red Lake Ojibwe [13][17]. The Five Tribes in Indian Territory were initially exempt. The Curtis Act of 1898 closed that door: it "helped weaken and dissolve Indian Territory tribal governments by abolishing tribal courts and subjecting all persons in the territory to federal law," required that "any tribal legislation passed after 1898 had to be approved by the president of the United States," and authorized the Dawes Commission "to prepare new citizenship rolls for each tribe" — with enrollment and allotment "performed without tribal consent" [18]. The Oklahoma Historical Society's summary is unsparing about the result: the act "completed the extinction of Indian land claims in the territory," violating "the promise of the United States that the Indian territory would remain Indian land in perpetuity" [17].

That is the circle closing. The Cherokee had been promised, in the Removal Act's §3, land "forever" in the west [6]. Sixty-eight years later, the Curtis Act extinguished their claims to it and enrolled them by a roll they did not consent to [17][18]. The perpetuity clause in §3 had a proviso the whole time — reversion "if the Indians become extinct, or abandon the same" [6]. Legal extinction, it turns out, did not require physical extinction.

**A note on fractionation, still live.** Allotment was a one-time division; inheritance kept subdividing. Interior's own account: "[w]ith each successive generation, ownership in allotted lands became increasingly divided among more heirs" [15]. The Buy-Back Program acquired over 1 million fractional interests, consolidating roughly 3 million equivalent acres at 53 locations; at its conclusion in November 2022, approximately 2.4 million fractional interests remained at 150 locations, comprising over 5.6 million equivalent acres worth several billion dollars [15]. The Dawes Act is not a historical event in this field. It is an unfunded administrative condition maintained by the federal government in the present.

## III. What the Pairing Shows

Both instruments proceed by the same logic, and the logic is the one *Johnson* stated: the United States holds ultimate title; Indigenous nations hold occupancy, which the United States alone may extinguish [12]. Removal exercised the extinguishing power directly. Allotment exercised it by changing the legal form of occupancy so that title passed through the channels of ordinary property law — purchase, homestead, inheritance — none of which required a treaty, a court order, or a march.

So the answer to the prompt's second half is not merely that reform rhetoric *disguised* the taking. It is that the rhetoric was **functionally necessary** to it. Removing nations by force in 1838 required federal power visible enough to be politically costly — which is why the removal era generated a sustained opposition, and why the reform movement of the 1880s could present itself as the Indians' friend against it. But the reform movement's humanitarian premise — that the tribe was the obstacle to the individual's progress — was *already* the premise that justified extinguishing tribal title. The Friends of the Indian believed they were dismantling a system of "enforced isolation, legalized pauperism and inevitable idleness" [14]. They were dismantling the communal landholding that was the material basis of Cherokee and Creek and Choctaw nationhood [17][18].

That is the through-line: **law converts a political program into a legal premise, and reform language supplies the moral warrant for the conversion.** *Worcester* is where we watch the first half happen. Dawes is where we watch the second.
