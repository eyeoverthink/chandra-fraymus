## 1. The Two Books, Side by Side

**Dunbar-Ortiz** writes a structural history of **settler colonialism** as an ongoing condition —
her frame is that US policy toward Indigenous nations was not a series of unfortunate frontier
excesses but a deliberate program to seize territory and displace or eliminate its inhabitants
[4]. Her table of contents shows the arc: Ch. 5 "The Birth of a Nation" opens at p. 78 and runs
toward p. 95, where Ch. 6, "The Last of the Mohicans and Andrew Jackson's White Republic,"
begins [5]. The Smithsonian's summary of the book states that this policy "reached its zenith
under President Andrew Jackson," and that its ruthlessness was articulated by US Army general
Thomas S. Jesup, who in 1836 wrote of the Seminoles: "The country can be rid of them only by
exterminating them." [4]

**Perdue & Green** write a focused narrative history of one nation's experience of that policy.
Their table of contents maps the same period onto Cherokee specifics — Ch. 3 "Indian Removal
Policy," Ch. 4 "Resisting Removal," Ch. 5 "The Treaty of New Echota," Ch. 6 "The Trail of Tears,"
Ch. 7 "Rebuilding in the West" [1].

**The productive friction for discussion:** a structural account of settler colonialism and a
narrative account of Cherokee statecraft are *not* interchangeable, and the difference is not
merely stylistic. Dunbar-Ortiz's unit of analysis is the settler state; Perdue & Green's is a
sovereign nation making strategic choices under coercion. Where Dunbar-Ortiz risks making
Cherokee agency disappear into structure, Perdue & Green risk making structure disappear into
individual decisions. Ask which account explains the Treaty of New Echota better.

## 3. *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) — What the Court Actually Held

Worcester, a missionary, was indicted for "residing, on the 15th July, 1831, in that part of the
Cherokee Nation attached by the laws of the State of Georgia to that County, without a license or
permit from the Governor." He pleaded that he was in Cherokee territory, outside Georgia's
jurisdiction, and that treaties had "acknowledged [the Cherokee Nation] to be a sovereign nation"
with territory "guaranteed to them by the United States." Georgia's Superior Court overruled the
plea; he was convicted and sentenced "to hard labour in the penitentiary for four years." [2]

The Court reached the merits and reversed. Two points carry the discussion:

**The "extraterritorial power" principle.** The Court held: "The extraterritorial power of every
legislature being limited in its action to its own citizens or subjects, the very passage of this
act is an assertion of jurisdiction over the Cherokee Nation, and of the rights and powers
consequent thereto." [2]

**The doctrine of discovery, accepted as law.** The opinion states as a principle that "discovery
of parts of the continent of America gave title to the government by whose subjects, or by whose
authority it was made, against all other European governments, which title might be consummated
by possession." [2] Note the structure: the same opinion that protects Cherokee jurisdiction
*rests* that protection on a doctrine asserting European-derived title. Dunbar-Ortiz devotes a
chapter to that doctrine, though it falls outside this week's reading `[unverified — Ch. 11 of her
book, not assigned]`.

**Compare the two cases.** A year earlier, in *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*, the Court described
the Cherokee as "domestic dependent nations" in "a state of pupilage," their relation to the
United States resembling "that of a ward to his guardian" — while simultaneously conceding the
Cherokees "are a State" and "have been uniformly treated as a State." [3] That contradiction is
your best single discussion lever: the same Court, one year apart, both denies Cherokee standing as
a foreign state and affirms that Georgia's laws cannot reach Cherokee territory.

## 4. The Indian Removal Act — Read the Language, Not the Label

Jackson described removal as "voluntary" [7]. The statute's own words reward close reading [6]:

- **Sec. 1** authorizes the President to divide western territory "for the reception of such
  tribes or nations of Indians as may choose to exchange the lands where they now reside."
- **Sec. 3** authorizes the President to "solemnly to assure the tribe or nation ... that the
  United States will forever secure and guaranty to them ... the country so exchanged with them" —
  with a proviso that "such lands shall revert to the United States, if the Indians become
  extinct, or abandon the same."
- **Sec. 4** values "improvements" and pays for them, after which "possession shall not afterwards
  be permitted to any of the same tribe."
- **Sec. 7** contains the safeguard: nothing in the act "shall be construed as authorizing or
  directing the violation of any existing treaty." [6]

Two things follow. First, the act's stated premise is consent — it "invites" exchange. Its
operative effect, as the National Constitution Center puts it, was that although some tribes
accepted, "others such as the Cherokees resisted," and during the Fall and Winter of 1838–39 the
Cherokee "were forcibly removed by the United States government in a march that later became known
as the Trail of Tears." [7] Second, Sec. 3's reverter clause plus Sec. 4's bar on future possession
mean the statute contemplated that the exchanging nation might cease to exist on that land.

The gap between a consent-based statute and a coerced removal is where the Treaty of New Echota
(Perdue & Green, Ch. 5 [1]) becomes the hinge of the whole story. For primary materials beyond the
statute itself, the Library of Congress maintains a research guide on the act and its after-effects
[10].

## Discussion Questions

Framed for open disagreement; each cites the source it hangs on.

**On structure vs. agency — the two-book problem**

1. Dunbar-Ortiz's unit of analysis is the settler state; Perdue & Green's is the Cherokee Nation as
   a political actor. If you take Perdue & Green seriously, does "settler colonialism" stop being
   an *explanation* and become only a description of context? Conversely, does their narrative
   make Cherokee resistance legible in a way a structural account cannot? [1][4]

2. The Smithsonian's summary of Dunbar-Ortiz says removal policy "reached its zenith under
   President Andrew Jackson." Is "zenith under Jackson" compatible with what she argues in Ch. 5
   about 1783 and the Treaty of Paris — that the genocidal program begins at nation-founding, not
   with Jackson? Which date is the real origin point, and why does it matter? [4][8]

**On *Worcester* and law as a tool**

3. *Worcester* protects Cherokee jurisdiction while grounding it in the doctrine of discovery —
   European title by "discovery" consummated by "possession." Can a sovereignty claim built on that
   foundation be stable? What does it mean to win a case that ratifies the premise of your
   dispossession? [2]

4. Compare the Court's two formulations, one year apart: "domestic dependent nations" in "a state
   of pupilage" (1831) [3] versus the holding that Georgia's laws cannot reach Cherokee territory
   (1832) [2]. Is this a contradiction, a strategic retreat, or a consistent doctrine of federal
   supremacy dressed in different clothes?

5. The Court decided *Worcester* in 1832. Georgia did not comply, and Jackson did not enforce it.
   What does that tell you about the limits of law as a protection for sovereignty — and about
   whether the Court's opinion should be read as a remedy or as a record? [2][7]

**On the Removal Act's language**

6. Sec. 3 promises the exchanged land will be secured "forever," with a proviso that it reverts "if
   the Indians become extinct, or abandon the same," and Sec. 7 forbids construing the act to
   violate existing treaties [6]. Was this bad faith at drafting, or a genuine contradiction
   between two competing legal principles that Congress never resolved? Which reading does the
   Trail of Tears vindicate?

7. Jackson called removal "voluntary" [7]; the statute's operative verb is "exchange" [6]. The
   Treaty of New Echota was signed by a minority faction and opposed by Principal Chief John Ross
   and the Cherokee government [1]. At what point does "consent" in the Removal Act framework
   become a legal fiction? Was New Echota a treaty or a ratification of a decision already made in
   Washington?

**On the Cherokee Nation and the Trail of Tears (Perdue & Green)**

8. Their chapter order is "Indian Removal Policy," then "Resisting Removal," *then* "The Treaty of
   New Echota," then "The Trail of Tears" [1]. Why put the treaty between resistance and removal?
   Does that sequence treat the treaty as the cause of removal or as its pretext? And how does that
   framing compare with Dunbar-Ortiz's structural account?

9. "Rebuilding in the West" is the final chapter [1]. Does ending with reconstruction risk
   normalizing removal as a hardship overcome — or is it a refusal to let the story end with
   victimization? Which choice would Dunbar-Ortiz make, given her emphasis on sustained Indigenous
   resistance [4]?

**Bring to the seminar (open to the room)**

10. Both books are acts of *reclaiming history* — Dunbar-Ortiz's subtitle is "Reclaiming History:
    Indigenous Voices in" ... [record truncated there] [4]. If history writing is itself a
    political act, what standards should we hold these books to? Whose evidence counts, and how
    should we weigh oral tradition against the treaty record and the Court's opinions?
