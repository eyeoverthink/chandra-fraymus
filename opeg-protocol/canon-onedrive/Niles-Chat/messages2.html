The argument I built

The Worcester contradiction isn't a paradox — it's the design. The standard telling is "court did right, was ignored." Reading the opinion shows the Court's protection of Cherokee sovereignty and the removal that followed came from the same premise. The holding rests on Marshall's Johnson v. McIntosh formulation: "discovery gave title to the government by whose subjects ... it was made," with Indigenous nations "admitted to be the rightful occupants of the soil" but their "rights to complete sovereignty ... necessarily diminished," and the U.S. holding "an exclusive right to extinguish the Indian title of occupancy." So the Cherokee got sovereignty residual to a title the Court had already assigned to the federal government. Worcester protected them from Georgia, not from the United States. Georgia's defiance is usually the moral of the story; that reading is too generous to the law, which had already conceded the dispositive question.

Dawes completed it by changing the legal form of holding. Removal moved nations to clear land; allotment dissolved the communal title that made land defensible, so land passed through ordinary property channels — purchase, homestead, inheritance — needing no treaty, court order, or march. From the statute itself: 25-year trust patent → fee simple; §5 making the unallotted remainder available "to actual settlers"; proceeds held "subject to appropriation by Congress for the education and civilization of such tribe."

And the reform language wasn't decoration — it was functionally necessary. Prof. C.C. Painter at Lake Mohonk in 1887 described the allottee stepping "out of his undifferentiated, impersonal tribal relation into one of individualized, responsible citizenship."
